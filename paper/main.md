abstract: |
  Graph analytics has poor locality, does little computation per byte, and has control flow that depends on the data. For these reasons, it is one of the clearest examples of the memory wall. Computer architects have responded with two competing approaches. Processing-in-memory (PIM) moves simple compute units into DRAM or next to it, so that data does not need to cross the memory bus. Domain-specific accelerators (DSAs) instead build the compute pipeline around the structure of the graph and around how the graph changes over time. This survey examines 34 works from 2015 to 2026 (hardware designs, real-hardware studies, position papers, and two software systems used as context), with a focus on 2021--2026. It compares the two camps on throughput, energy, and memory bandwidth, and states the baseline and the evaluation method (simulation, field-programmable gate array (FPGA) emulation, or silicon) of every reported number. Almost no study compares the two camps under matched conditions, so we do not rank them. Instead, we ask which assumptions each camp's results depend on. Graph size alone does not separate the camps, because its benefit for PIM depends on communication. On current hardware, one assumption does separate them: whether communication between units must pass through a host. Access granularity affects both camps and explains why they are converging. Graph mutability marks a gap in coverage: in the venues we searched, every PIM and hybrid design assumes a static graph, while DSAs already support streaming and evolving graphs. All silicon evidence for PIM comes from one commercial platform whose architecture differs from the simulated designs, so its weaker results cannot be blamed on simulation alone. Finally, we discuss security, dependability, and predictability, which the literature has mostly not studied, and six open research directions.

# Introduction

Graph algorithms such as breadth-first search (BFS), single-source shortest paths (SSSP), PageRank, and subgraph pattern matching are used in many applications, from social-network analysis to bioinformatics and network security [@ahn2015tesseract; @ham2016graphicionado; @asquini2025tc]. These algorithms run poorly on conventional processors. Each vertex or edge needs only a few operations, and the next address often depends on the value that was just loaded. As a result, caches and prefetchers have little to work with. Tesseract's motivation study shows the size of this problem. On a four-socket, 32-core server with 640 GB/s of memory bandwidth, ideal execution of five graph workloads would need close to 500 GB/s of mostly random traffic. The authors note that pin-limited systems cannot deliver this bandwidth for random access [@ahn2015tesseract]. Caches do not solve the problem. Piccolo measures that more than 90% of the bytes that a cache fetches during BFS are evicted before they are used. The reason is that one useful vertex of 4--8 B arrives inside a 64 B burst [@shin2025piccolo]. When so little of each transfer is useful, adding more bandwidth or capacity to a conventional memory hierarchy gives smaller and smaller gains. Architects therefore try either to reduce the distance that data travels or to increase the useful part of each transfer.

Two architectural responses have developed in parallel. The first is *processing-in-memory* (PIM). It places compute units in the logic layer of 3D-stacked memory, in the buffer chips of dynamic random-access memory (DRAM), next to DRAM banks, or inside resistive crossbars. Data then moves over short internal paths instead of the external bus [@ahn2015tesseract; @song2018graphr; @gomezluna2022prim]. The second is *domain-specific acceleration*, which uses domain-specific accelerators (DSAs). This approach keeps the memory conventional and builds specialized pipelines, schedulers, and on-chip storage around the vertex-centric execution model [@ham2016graphicionado; @mukkara2018hats; @yan2019graphdyns]. A 2019 survey of graph accelerators concluded that there was "not an absolute winner" across preprocessing, parallel computation, and runtime scheduling [@gui2019survey]. Since then, the field has changed a lot. Researchers have characterized commercial PIM hardware (UPMEM) on real workloads [@gomezluna2022prim; @barkhordar2025alphapim]. DSAs now support streaming and evolving graphs [@rahman2021jetstream; @zhao2022tdgraph; @gao2023mega]. Hybrid designs in which the host and the memory cooperate, an idea that goes back to PEI and GraphPIM [@ahn2015pei; @nai2017graphpim], have returned in a refined form [@shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf]. This survey asks the following question:

> *For irregular graph workloads, how do PIM architectures and DSAs trade off throughput, energy efficiency, and effective memory bandwidth, and which workload and system assumptions decide when each is better?*

We divide this question into four sub-questions. **SQ1**: Which bottleneck does each camp address, and how have its designs developed over time? **SQ2**: What do the reported results show once their baselines and evaluation substrates are made explicit? **SQ3**: Which assumptions do the results of each camp depend on, and which of them separate the camps? **SQ4**: How do the camps differ in security, dependability, and predictability? Our contributions are the following: (i) a taxonomy of 34 works, based on where the compute is placed in the memory hierarchy (Section [2](#sec:background){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:background"}); (ii) a review of each camp that follows how its designs developed and reports every headline number together with its baseline (Sections [3](#sec:pim){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:pim"}--[5](#sec:hybrid){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:hybrid"}); (iii) a comparative analysis of the assumptions behind each camp's results (Section [6](#sec:analysis){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:analysis"}); and (iv) an assessment of the properties that matter for critical systems, and of open challenges (Sections [7](#sec:critical){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:critical"}--[8](#sec:open){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:open"}). We use "DSA" broadly: it includes stand-alone accelerators and engines attached to each core. Graph neural network accelerators, GPU-only systems, and near-storage graph processing are outside our scope.

Section [2](#sec:background){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:background"} introduces the workload model, the taxonomy, and our method. Sections [3](#sec:pim){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:pim"}--[5](#sec:hybrid){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:hybrid"} review the PIM, DSA, and hybrid designs. Section [6](#sec:analysis){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:analysis"} compares the camps, Section [7](#sec:critical){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:critical"} evaluates properties for critical systems, Section [8](#sec:open){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:open"} lists open challenges, and the last section concludes.

# Background and Survey Method {#sec:background}

## Graph workloads

Most of the surveyed designs use the vertex-centric model. In each iteration, the active vertices read their edges and send updates to their neighbors (*scatter* or *push*). The updates are then combined into the properties of the destination vertices (*apply*) [@ham2016graphicionado; @yenimol2026cograf]. Graphs are usually stored in compressed sparse row (CSR) or compressed sparse column form. These formats are good for reading edges in sequence, but they make insertion expensive. Coordinate (COO) lists are easy to extend but harder to traverse [@asquini2025tc]. Traversal workloads (BFS, SSSP) activate a small *frontier* of vertices that changes in each iteration. PageRank-style workloads touch every vertex. Graph mining, in contrast, consists mostly of set intersections and unions over neighbor lists [@besta2021sisa]. Two properties appear in every design we survey. First, vertex properties are small (4--8 B) compared with the 64 B DRAM burst, so most of a cache line fetched for one vertex is wasted [@shin2025piccolo]. Second, the degree distributions of real-world graphs are skewed. This makes both partitions and crossbar mappings unbalanced [@zheng2020spara; @cai2024pimpam].

Two choices in the execution model also appear often and directly affect hardware design. The first choice is *synchronization*. Bulk-synchronous designs, such as Graphicionado, process all active vertices of an iteration before they start the next one [@ham2016graphicionado]. Asynchronous and event-driven designs send updates as soon as they are produced. JetStream builds on such an event-driven accelerator, which its authors report was up to $6\times$ faster than a bulk-synchronous design [@rahman2021jetstream]. GraphQ's multi-node model also accepts values that are out of date by a bounded amount [@zhuo2019graphq]. The second choice is *direction*. In push-based execution, a vertex writes updates into its neighbors. This creates scattered writes, which PIM can handle in place. In pull-based execution, a vertex reads from its neighbors, and this does not map as easily onto PIM. For this reason, CoGraf, for example, evaluates only push-based variants [@yenimol2026cograf]. GraphDynS describes the resulting difficulties as three kinds of irregularity [@yan2019graphdyns]. *Workload* irregularity means that threads have different numbers of edges. *Traversal* irregularity means that the number of edges per iteration is unpredictable, and that atomic conflicts occur when many edges update the same vertex. *Update* irregularity means that the set of updated and activated vertices changes in every iteration, but all vertices are still checked. Most papers report speedup and energy relative to a baseline, but some report traversed edges per second (TEPS) or TEPS per watt [@lee2022graphwave]. This also makes direct comparison hard.

The surveyed papers themselves address five recurring research questions. How can communication between memory units be reduced (GraphP, GraphQ, Dalorex)? How can locality be recovered without offline preprocessing (HATS, Piccolo, FALA)? How should hardware handle graph updates (JetStream, TDGraph, MEGA)? How much flexibility does a graph accelerator need (PolyGraph)? And how does PIM behave on real hardware (PrIM and the UPMEM studies)? Our own sub-questions compare the answers across camps.

## Where does the compute sit?

We classify designs by the level of the memory hierarchy where the graph computation runs (Fig. [\[fig:placement\]](#fig:placement){reference-type="ref" reference="fig:placement"}). *On-chip DSAs* sit on the processor side of the memory bus. They are either stand-alone accelerators or units attached to cores [@ham2016graphicionado; @mukkara2018hats; @zhao2022tdgraph]. *Processing-near-memory* (PnM) places programmable cores or fixed-function units in the logic layer of a 3D stack [@ahn2015tesseract; @zhuo2019graphq], in the buffer chip of a dual in-line memory module (DIMM) [@talati2022ndminer], or next to each DRAM bank, as in UPMEM [@gomezluna2022prim]. PIM can also work at the level of single instructions. The host sends simple operations, such as atomic updates, to compute units in the memory and keeps control of the data layout [@ahn2015pei; @nai2017graphpim]. *Fine-grained* or *bank-level* PIM adds simple arithmetic logic units (ALUs) that operate on whole DRAM columns inside the banks [@yenimol2026cograf; @shin2025fala]. *Processing-using-memory* computes inside the memory array itself, either with digital bulk bitwise operations in DRAM, as SISA uses [@besta2021sisa], or with resistive crossbars that perform analog sparse matrix--vector multiplication [@song2018graphr; @zheng2020spara; @challapalle2020gaasx]. The deeper the compute is placed, the more internal bandwidth it can use. However, deeper placement also brings stronger limits on precision, programmability, and communication. One design fits neither camp. Dalorex keeps the whole graph in the on-chip memory of a large grid of small tiles, and each task runs on the tile that holds its data [@orenesvera2023dalorex]. We treat it as a point between the camps.

## Survey method

We built the corpus through targeted searches with a general web search engine, the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, arXiv, dblp, and author pages. The queries combined keywords such as "processing-in-memory graph", "near-data processing graph analytics", "graph processing accelerator", "ReRAM graph accelerator", "streaming/dynamic graph accelerator", and "HBM graph accelerator" with the names of ISCA, MICRO, HPCA, ASPLOS, and DSN and the years 2021--2026. Separate queries covered fault tolerance, security, and predictability of graph accelerators. The main search ended on 28 September 2026, and gap-closing searches ran on 11 October 2026. We verified each entry against a publisher, dblp, institutional, or author record. We included a design if it proposes or evaluates PIM, near-data, or domain-specific hardware for graph analytics, traversal, or mining. We also included important designs from before 2021 when later work compares against them. Four works (PEI, GraphPIM, PolyGraph, and Dalorex) were added after a review of an earlier draft pointed them out. ScalaGraph [@yao2022scalagraph], also pointed out, is not included because we could not obtain its full text. We read all but two of the included works in full text, and we took every reported number from the paper itself. The two exceptions, MGX and NDMiner, are cited only for claims that appear in their abstracts. One FPGA design (ReGraph [@chen2022regraph]) serves as a reference point, because our comparison is about PIM and application-specific integrated circuit (ASIC) design approaches. Two software systems, CommonGraph [@afarin2023commongraph] and Glign [@yin2023glign], are cited as algorithmic context and are not counted as hardware. Table [\[tab:corpus\]](#tab:corpus){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:corpus"} summarizes the corpus.

*Limitations of the search.* First, we did not log how many results each query returned or how many papers we screened out, so we cannot report a full identification and screening flow. Second, the corpus contains more PIM works than DSA works. One reason is that we searched outside the target venues for real-hardware PIM studies, and all four UPMEM studies come from other venues. We did not run the same wider search for real-hardware DSAs. The imbalance is therefore not evidence that one camp is more active. Third, our absence claims about hardware designs refer to ISCA, MICRO, HPCA, and ASPLOS in 2021--2026. We searched DSN only for the critical-system properties. "Absent" means "not found in our search"; it does not mean that no such work exists.

*Threats to validity.* All numbers are as reported by the authors of each paper, and we did not reproduce any result. One surveyed work reports that it could not reproduce another's simulated results (Section [6](#sec:analysis){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:analysis"}). A single author extracted the data with the help of AI tools, and then checked every number against the full text. The extraction sheet is kept with the sources of this paper.

*Comparison rule.* The surveyed papers report speedups against single-threaded CPUs, multi-threaded frameworks, GPUs, or earlier accelerators. They use different datasets and different substrates (cycle-level simulation, FPGA emulation, or silicon). For this reason, we never place two numbers side by side unless they use the same class of baseline and the same substrate. Every number in Table [\[tab:designs\]](#tab:designs){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:designs"} is given together with its baseline.

# Processing-in-Memory for Graphs {#sec:pim}

## 3D-stacked PIM: the communication problem

Tesseract set the template for graph PIM [@ahn2015tesseract]. It places a simple in-order core in each vault of a Hybrid Memory Cube (HMC). Instead of reading remote data, Tesseract moves the computation to the data through *remote function calls*. These calls are non-blocking, so they hide latency, and they make updates atomic without software locks. Two specialized prefetchers use hints from the programming interface to keep the simple cores busy. With 16 cubes, the cores have 8 TB/s of total internal bandwidth, compared with 640 GB/s for the baseline server. Tesseract reports about $10\times$ higher performance and 87% lower energy than this baseline on graphs with four to seven million vertices [@ahn2015tesseract]. The key property is that bandwidth grows with memory capacity: adding cubes adds both storage and compute.

Later work shows where this scaling stops. In Tesseract's vertex-programming model, each edge between two cubes produces a remote message. These messages travel over serializer/deserializer (SerDes) links, whose bandwidth is far lower than the total internal bandwidth of the cubes. GraphP therefore treats data organization as a main design concern. Its "source-cut" partitioning sends one update per replica instead of one per edge. This gives a $1.7\times$ speedup and 89% energy saving *over Tesseract* in cycle-accurate simulation [@zhang2018graphp]. GraphQ goes further. It reorders vertex processing so that messages between cubes are batched, structured, and overlapped with computation, and it uses different types of cores for different types of access. The bandwidth between nodes is even lower than the bandwidth between cubes. GraphQ therefore adds a hybrid execution model in which nodes do extra local computation while they communicate with other nodes. This model works for asynchronous iterative algorithms that accept values that are out of date by a bounded amount [@zhuo2019graphq]. GraphQ reports $3.3\times$ on average ($13.9\times$ maximum) and 81% energy saving over Tesseract in zSim [@zhuo2019graphq]. Its often-quoted $98.34\times$ figure compares a four-node GraphQ with a *single node that uses a conventional memory hierarchy*. This figure shows that performance scales with capacity. It is not a gain over earlier PIM [@zhuo2019graphq]. Each step removes a communication problem left by the previous design, while the compute capability hardly changes.

## 3D-stacked PIM: offloading single instructions

A second line of 3D-stacked PIM keeps the normal programming model. PEI adds PIM-enabled instructions that run simple operations either in the memory or on the host. A hardware monitor chooses the location from the locality of the data. For large inputs, PEI is 47% faster than the same system without PIM. For small inputs, it keeps the performance of that system, while always running the operations in memory would lose 20% [@ahn2015pei]. GraphPIM applies this idea to graph frameworks. It offloads atomic updates of vertex properties to the atomic operations of the HMC 2.0 specification, without changes to user code or to the instruction set. Compared with a conventional system that uses HMC as main memory, it reports up to $2.4\times$ speedup and 37% less energy [@nai2017graphpim]. GraphPIM also shows a limit of this approach. It classifies graph construction, graph update, and topology changes as not suitable for offloading, because they need operations more complex than the simple atomics that HMC provides [@nai2017graphpim]. These two designs show that locality-aware cooperation between host and memory is an old idea. The hybrids of Section [5](#sec:hybrid){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:hybrid"} refine it.

## Analog resistive RAM (ReRAM) PIM: less precision for less energy

GraphR takes a different approach. If a vertex program can be written as sparse matrix--vector multiplication (SpMV), small blocks of the graph can be stored in ReRAM crossbars and processed in the analog domain [@song2018graphr]. GraphR reports a geometric-mean speedup of $16.01\times$ (maximum $132.67\times$) and $33.82\times$ energy saving over a CPU baseline. It also reports a $1.69$--$2.19\times$ speedup over a GPU and a $1.16$--$4.12\times$ speedup over a PIM architecture [@song2018graphr]. Its weakness is sparsity. In real graphs, most crossbar cells hold zeros, and because activations depend on the input, many cycles do no useful work. Spara maps source vertices randomly to wordlines and destination vertices consecutively to bitlines. It also adds a bank-parallel architecture that keeps the crossbars densely filled. Spara reports $8.21\times$ and $5.01\times$ higher performance and $8.97\times$ and $5.68\times$ energy savings over GraphR and GraphSAR, respectively. Its lightweight reordering adds only 3.9--6.6% extra preprocessing time on the largest graph evaluated [@zheng2020spara]. This shows a common trade-off in the analog class: the efficiency of the hardware depends on software preprocessing, which must be repeated every time the graph changes. GaaS-X supports sparse formats directly. It uses content-addressable crossbars to avoid converting sparse data to dense data and to avoid redundant writes. It reports $7.7\times$ and $2.4\times$ higher performance and $22\times$ and $5.7\times$ energy savings over GraphR and GRAM [@challapalle2020gaasx]. The whole class can only run SpMV-expressible kernels, and its precision is limited by analog-to-digital conversion. GraphR argues that iterative graph algorithms tolerate this imprecision [@song2018graphr]. We return to this claim in Section [7](#sec:critical){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:critical"}.

## Set-centric mining and synchronization support

Graph mining puts different demands on PIM than traversal does. SISA observes that set intersections and unions dominate many mining algorithms. It offers these operations as instruction-set architecture (ISA) extensions and maps them to two kinds of PIM. In-DRAM bulk bitwise operations handle the dense neighbor sets of high-degree vertices, and logic-layer cores handle the sparse integer arrays of low-degree vertices. Between 5% and 30% of the largest neighborhoods are stored as bitvectors and the rest as integer arrays, so the data representation itself adapts to the skewed degrees. SISA reports more than $10\times$ speedup over a non-SISA implementation of the established Bron--Kerbosch maximal-clique algorithm [@besta2021sisa]. SISA thus combines two PIM placements in one design and chooses between them by vertex degree. The hybrids in Section [5](#sec:hybrid){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:hybrid"} apply this idea to the split between host and memory. NDMiner places set-operation units in the DRAM buffer chip. It also adds hardware that skips loads that symmetry-breaking constraints make unnecessary. Its abstract reports gains of $6.4\times$ and $2.5\times$ over software and hardware baselines [@talati2022ndminer]. Every parallel PIM design also needs synchronization without cache coherence. SynCron provides synchronization engines near the data and reports improvements of $1.27\times$ (high contention) and $1.35\times$ (low-contention real applications) over earlier schemes [@giannoula2021syncron].

## Real hardware: results on UPMEM

Until recently, the evidence for PIM came from simulation or, at best, from simplified hardware prototypes [@gomezluna2022prim]. UPMEM's commercial PIM DIMMs changed this. Each DRAM processing unit (DPU) is an in-order core with 24 hardware threads and exclusive access to a 64 MB bank and a 64 KB scratchpad. It runs at 350 MHz in the 2,556-DPU system and at 267 MHz in the 640-DPU system that PrIM evaluates. The most important limit is that *DPUs cannot communicate directly*. All data exchanged between DPUs moves through the host CPU over the memory bus [@gomezluna2022prim]. The PrIM characterization found that a system with 2,556 DPUs is $93.0\times$ faster than a modern CPU on average. This result covers the 13 benchmarks that need neither heavy synchronization between DPUs nor intensive floating-point operations. A system with 640 DPUs is $27.9\times$ faster. However, the smaller system is slower than a GPU on most benchmarks [@gomezluna2022prim]. BFS is one of the benchmarks that do need this synchronization. After every BFS iteration, the host merges the frontiers of all DPUs one after another and sends them back. For this reason, the synchronization time grows with the number of DPUs [@gomezluna2022prim].

Graph studies on UPMEM confirm this. PimPam achieves a $22.5\times$ average speedup over the GraphPi CPU framework for pattern matching [@cai2024pimpam]. Its four main optimizations are revealing: task assignment based on load, space-efficient parallel partitioning that fits each DPU's part of the graph into its 64 MB bank, adaptive multithreading, and dynamic bitmaps. Each of them works around a limit of the platform (little memory per core, no communication between cores, weak in-order cores). None of them exploits a property of the graph [@cai2024pimpam]. ALPHA-PIM rewrites BFS, SSSP, and personalized PageRank as sparse matrix--sparse vector products. It finds that the best way to partition the data depends on the density of the input vector and on the dataset. For BFS, it measures a DPU compute utilization of 18.5% inside the kernel but only 5% end to end. This is still far above the 0.01--0.05% that it measures on CPU and GPU. The authors ask for better direct memory access (DMA) engines, more instruction-level parallelism, and direct interconnects between PIM cores [@barkhordar2025alphapim]. A 2025 workshop paper on triangle counting uses vertex coloring to avoid communication between DPUs, reservoir sampling to fit the data into the bank, and a Misra--Gries summary to handle high-degree vertices. Even so, it finds that UPMEM is generally slower than CPU (CSR) and GPU implementations on static graphs [@asquini2025tc]. PIM is faster than the CPU only when updates arrive as COO batches that are appended to the graph, because these batches force the CPU to rebuild its CSR. The real-hardware evidence therefore limits the conclusions from simulation. The bandwidth benefit is real, but communication through the host limits it, exactly for the frontier-driven traversals that motivate graph PIM.

# Domain-Specific Accelerators {#sec:dsa}

## Pipelines for static graphs

Graphicionado is the reference design for graph DSAs [@ham2016graphicionado]. It maps the vertex-programming model onto a reconfigurable pipeline that is specialized for vertex and edge data types. The key change on the memory side is that random updates to destination vertices go to a large on-chip scratchpad instead of off-chip DRAM. The pipeline cannot be scaled simply by copying it, because copies that read and write the same scratchpad locations are serialized. Graphicionado therefore splits the pipeline into a part for source vertices and a part for destination vertices. It replicates each part separately and sends edges between them through a crossbar, based on the destination vertex [@ham2016graphicionado]. Graphicionado reports a $1.76$--$6.54\times$ speedup and $50$--$100\times$ lower energy than a 32-thread software framework on a 16-core Xeon. It was evaluated with register-transfer level (RTL) synthesis and DRAMSim2 [@ham2016graphicionado].

HATS takes a different approach. Its authors argue that real graphs have locality in their community structure, but that this locality is hard to predict in advance. They also argue that reordering the graph offline costs more than it saves. HATS therefore implements bounded depth-first scheduling (BDFS) as a small hardware unit next to each core. BDFS makes each core explore a small, well-connected region depth-first, up to a fixed depth. This improves temporal locality on graphs with community structure. The paper compares a vertex-ordered engine (VO-HATS) with BDFS-HATS. On PageRank Delta over the uk-2002 graph, they improve performance by $1.8\times$ and $2.7\times$, respectively. In zsim, BDFS reduces main-memory accesses by up to $2.4\times$ (30% on average) without any preprocessing [@mukkara2018hats]. HATS shows that a DSA can recover locality *online*, which matters for dynamic graphs, where offline reordering is never complete. It is also cheap: 0.14 mm$^2$ and 72 mW at 65 nm, very small compared with a general-purpose core [@mukkara2018hats]. Locality-aware scheduling therefore does not need a stand-alone accelerator. TDGraph's per-core engines follow the same approach, and TDGraph uses HATS as one of its baselines [@zhao2022tdgraph].

GraphDynS identifies data-dependent behavior as the root cause of irregularity. Its Dispatching/Processing programming model decouples the datapath, so that data dependencies can be found at runtime. A data-aware scheduler then uses these dependencies to schedule workloads, data accesses, and computations on the fly. The authors show that this addresses all three kinds of irregularity. The design is implemented in RTL and evaluated with five algorithms on six real-world and five synthetic graphs [@yan2019graphdyns]. It reports $4.4\times$ speedup and $11.6\times$ less energy than a GPGPU solution *while using half the memory bandwidth*. It also reports $1.9\times$ speedup and $1.8\times$ less energy than an earlier graph accelerator with *the same* bandwidth [@yan2019graphdyns]. GraphDynS therefore attacks irregularity at its source, while Graphicionado and HATS work around its effects. PolyGraph asks how much flexibility a graph accelerator needs. Its template architecture supports several algorithm variants, such as synchronous or asynchronous updates and different policies for scheduling vertices. In cycle-level simulation, the best single variant is $1.93\times$ faster than GraphPulse, the best earlier accelerator. Choosing the variant for each workload adds another $2.71\times$, and switching variants between phases adds up to 50% [@dadu2021polygraph]. PolyGraph therefore shows that the algorithm variant matters as much as the hardware. GraphWave targets edge devices. Its authors call it compute-at-memory, but we count it with the on-chip DSAs because its compute units and data sit together on the accelerator chip. It assigns one functional unit to every vertex and optimizes the on-chip multicast network. It reaches 97.8 GTEPS at 63.94 GTEPS/W in gate-level simulation, but the number of on-chip units limits the size of the graphs it supports [@lee2022graphwave].

## An FPGA reference point

ReGraph shows what happens when a DSA has PIM-like bandwidth through a High Bandwidth Memory (HBM) package [@chen2022regraph]. On an Alveo U280 with 32 HBM channels (460 GB/s), monolithic pipeline designs run out of logic resources long before they can use all the channels. ReGraph therefore classifies partitions as dense or sparse. It pairs "Little" pipelines for the dense partitions with "Big" pipelines for the sparse partitions, which have poor locality, and it uses model-guided scheduling. It reports $1.6$--$5.9\times$ higher performance and $12\times$ better resource efficiency than earlier FPGA designs [@chen2022regraph]. ReGraph is the only design in our DSA set that runs completely on real hardware, so its numbers include effects that simulation usually leaves out, such as routing and resource limits. It shows that once external bandwidth is plentiful, the DSA bottleneck moves to the number of pipelines that fit on the chip and the differences between partitions.

## Streaming and evolving graphs

The most distinctive DSA development after 2021 concerns graphs that change. JetStream extends the event-driven GraphPulse accelerator to support edge additions, deletions, and updates. It reuses earlier query results and recomputes only the affected vertices. It supports both accumulative algorithms (e.g., PageRank) and monotonic algorithms (e.g., SSSP, where deletions can invalidate values that were already settled). It also limits memory accesses to the affected vertices. JetStream reports 90% less computation time than recomputing from scratch, and about $18\times$ speedup over the KickStarter and GraphBolt software frameworks, with larger gains for smaller batches [@rahman2021jetstream]. Small batches, which latency-sensitive uses need, are therefore where incremental hardware helps most.

TDGraph attaches a topology-driven engine to each core of a many-core processor. The engine sits between the core and its private L2 cache. It works on virtual addresses through the core's translation lookaside buffer (TLB), so it shares the conventional memory hierarchy instead of replacing it [@zhao2022tdgraph]. It starts the incremental computation from the vertices that the updates touch, and it prefetches along the graph topology, so that the propagations from many updates are merged. In a simulated 64-core system, it reports gains of $7.1$--$21.4\times$ over a software streaming system and $4.6$--$12.7\times$ over HATS, at an area cost of 0.73% [@zhao2022tdgraph].

MEGA targets *evolving* graphs, where one query must be evaluated on many snapshots of the graph. It builds on an idea from the CommonGraph software system: converting deletions into additions against a common base graph avoids expensive deletions [@afarin2023commongraph]. MEGA adds batch-oriented execution, so that snapshots that share batches of updates are evaluated together. It reports $24$--$120\times$ over CommonGraph and $4.08$--$5.98\times$ over JetStream [@gao2023mega]. At each of these three steps, the control logic of the accelerator grows to track what changed, while the memory system stays conventional. Concurrent queries over a shared graph cause a related problem: their traversals are not aligned. So far, this problem has been addressed in software, by Glign, which aligns concurrent traversals to recover shared locality [@yin2023glign].

# Convergence: Host--Memory Cooperation Revisited {#sec:hybrid}

Three recent designs return to the host--memory cooperation of PEI and GraphPIM (Section [3](#sec:pim){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:pim"}) and add DSA-style tiling. They argue that a pure DSA wastes bandwidth on coarse DRAM bursts, and that a pure PIM design cannot use the data reuse that tiling provides [@shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala]. Piccolo starts from a DSA and uses GraphDynS as its baseline accelerator [@shin2025piccolo]. It targets the burst waste described in Section I: a 64 B DDR burst for a 4--8 B vertex property. Instead of placing arithmetic units in memory, Piccolo adds *fine-grained in-memory scatter-gather*, so that tiles fetch only useful vertex data. It reports a $1.62\times$ geometric-mean speedup (up to $3.28\times$) over its strongest baseline, a cache-based GraphDynS, and up to 59.7% energy reduction. The in-memory mechanism is validated by FPGA emulation, and performance is measured with cycle-level simulation based on Ramulator [@shin2025piccolo].

FALA, from the same group, combines three mechanisms. First, a lightweight unit in each HBM2 bank performs the reduce phase of the vertex program. Second, a fine-grained column-access mechanism fetches several non-contiguous 8 B columns from one open row. Third, a locality-aware offloading policy sends each access either to the host cache or to PIM, depending on its locality, which comes from skewed degree distributions. FALA reports $1.56\times$ speedup and 35.3% energy reduction (geometric mean) over the best earlier design. It is evaluated against Piccolo, PEI, and GraphPIM [@shin2025fala].

CoGraf targets fine-grained PIM (FGPIM) for push-based vertex programs. A tuple-based last-level cache combines (coalesces) updates so that they match the column parallelism of DRAM. Once the update phase is faster, the apply phase becomes the bottleneck. CoGraf addresses this with bank-parallel predicated PIM instructions. It reports $4.4\times$/$9.8\times$ speedups over a state-of-the-art non-PIM baseline on HBM2/DDR4, and 88%/94% lower DRAM energy. These figures are measured over the region of interest, not end to end [@yenimol2026cograf].

The hybrids also respond to a cost argument. Piccolo deliberately does not place arithmetic units in DRAM. Its authors state that this is expensive and that supporting several operation types there is impractical [@shin2025piccolo]. FALA notes that commercial DRAM-PIM products with parallel arithmetic units have been reported to add up to 50% area overhead to the DRAM die. It also points out that many earlier PIM proposals assumed separate logic dies with serial interconnects, which do not map onto monolithic DRAM-PIM [@shin2025fala]. The hybrids therefore put only the minimum function in memory (scatter-gather, fine-grained column selection, simple reduction), and they keep control and data reuse on the processor side.

The hybrids matter for the research question in two ways. First, they show that the camps are coming together. DSAs bring locality management in caches and tiles, and PIM brings internal bandwidth and fine access granularity. The hybrids also inherit one limit from each camp: they depend on tiling and cache reuse, and they need modified memory parts. Second, all three keep the static-graph assumption: none of their full texts mentions dynamic, streaming, or evolving graphs [@shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf].

Dalorex reaches a similar goal by a different route. It distributes the graph evenly over a grid of small tiles, each with its own SRAM, so that all memory accesses are local. Tasks run on the tile that holds their data, and a network built for irregular traffic carries one-way messages between tiles [@orenesvera2023dalorex]. In cycle-level simulation with the same number of cores (256), Dalorex reports two orders of magnitude better performance and energy than Tesseract. It also ran PolyGraph's own code and found that PolyGraph stops scaling beyond 16 cores, because it then saturates the 512 GB/s of its HBM [@orenesvera2023dalorex]. Dalorex assumes that the whole graph fits in the combined on-chip memory (4.2 MB per tile in the Tesseract comparison), and, like the other designs in this section, a static graph.

# Comparative Analysis {#sec:analysis}

## Throughput

Both camps report large throughput gains, but their baselines cannot be compared. PIM gains are largest against conventional hosts. Tesseract reports $10\times$ over a 32-core server [@ahn2015tesseract], and GraphR reports $16.01\times$ over a CPU [@song2018graphr]. When PIM designs are compared with each other, the average speedups are single-digit, such as GraphQ's $3.3\times$ over Tesseract [@zhuo2019graphq] and FALA's $1.56\times$ over the strongest earlier design [@shin2025fala]. DSAs report $1.76$--$6.54\times$ over multithreaded software [@ham2016graphicionado] and $1.9\times$ over an earlier accelerator with the same bandwidth [@yan2019graphdyns]. On average, recent designs in both camps therefore improve on the best earlier design of their own camp by a single-digit factor. Maximum gains can be larger, such as $13.9\times$ for GraphQ. No study in our corpus compares a DRAM-based PIM design and a DSA under matched conditions. The closest evidence comes from two designs outside the pure camps. Piccolo, a hybrid, reports a $1.62\times$ speedup over a DSA baseline (GraphDynS) [@shin2025piccolo]. Dalorex compares itself with both a PIM design (Tesseract, at equal core count) and a DSA (PolyGraph), and outperforms both, but it is neither PIM nor a classic DSA [@orenesvera2023dalorex].

## Energy

Both camps report large energy savings, but again against different baseline classes, so the numbers do not show which camp saves more. Within PIM, savings against other PIM designs are reported: GraphP uses 89% less energy than Tesseract [@zhang2018graphp]. Against host systems, Tesseract reports 87% less energy, and CoGraf reports 88--94% less DRAM energy than its non-PIM baseline [@ahn2015tesseract; @yenimol2026cograf]. Analog ReRAM reports even larger savings, but it is less general: GraphR saves $33.82\times$ over a CPU, and GaaS-X saves $22\times$ over GraphR [@song2018graphr; @challapalle2020gaasx]. Within the DSA camp, Graphicionado uses $50$--$100\times$ less energy than multithreaded software [@ham2016graphicionado], and GraphDynS uses $11.6\times$ less than a GPU [@yan2019graphdyns]. What the two camps share is a mechanism argument, not a measured ranking. DSA savings come mostly from *removing general-purpose overheads*: instruction processing, cache misses, and wasted burst bandwidth. PIM savings come mostly from *removing external transfers*, because data movement is a large part of the energy cost in memory-bound workloads [@gomezluna2022prim]. The two mechanisms complement each other, and this explains why the hybrids combine them.

## Memory bandwidth

The camps are limited by different kinds of bandwidth. A DSA is limited by the external interface: pin bandwidth for DDR and channel count for HBM. Its design effort goes into getting more useful bytes from each transferred byte, through scratchpads, locality-aware scheduling, and separate handling of dense and sparse partitions [@ham2016graphicionado; @mukkara2018hats; @chen2022regraph]. Dalorex's experiment with PolyGraph's code shows this limit directly: PolyGraph stops scaling once it saturates its 512 GB/s of HBM [@orenesvera2023dalorex]. A PIM design has a lot of internal bandwidth, 8 TB/s in Tesseract's configuration [@ahn2015tesseract], but it is limited by the bandwidth *between units*. In HMC, this means the SerDes links, which motivated GraphP and GraphQ [@zhang2018graphp; @zhuo2019graphq]. In UPMEM, it means the host itself, which carries all traffic between DPUs [@gomezluna2022prim]. Graph traversals exchange frontiers between partitions, so the main limit for PIM is the fraction of edges that cross partitions. The main limit for DSAs is the fraction of each burst that is useful. Access granularity connects the two. The mismatch between burst size and property size that limits DSAs [@shin2025piccolo] is the same problem that fine-grained column access addresses on the PIM side [@shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf].

## How comparable is the evidence?

Table [\[tab:designs\]](#tab:designs){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:designs"} shows that almost all of the evidence comes from simulation. Among the hardware designs surveyed, only the UPMEM studies measure silicon, and only ReGraph and Piccolo's in-memory mechanism run on FPGA hardware [@gomezluna2022prim; @cai2024pimpam; @barkhordar2025alphapim; @asquini2025tc; @chen2022regraph; @shin2025piccolo]. The silicon results are weaker than the simulated ones. Simulated 3D-stacked PIM assumes efficient message passing between cubes [@ahn2015tesseract; @zhuo2019graphq], but on UPMEM the host limits BFS synchronization [@gomezluna2022prim], and BFS utilization drops to 5% end to end [@barkhordar2025alphapim]. This difference does not isolate a gap between simulation and silicon. UPMEM places simple cores beside DIMM banks with no interconnect between them, while the simulated designs place cores in an HMC logic layer with links between cubes. Architecture and evaluation method change together, so we cannot say how much of the difference comes from each. Simulation also has a reproducibility problem of its own. Dalorex followed GraphQ's methodology but could not reproduce its results: it obtained $3.2\times$ longer runtime and $4.6\times$ more energy (geometric mean) than GraphQ reported [@orenesvera2023dalorex]. Simulated DSAs are also optimistic in their own way. GraphWave's per-vertex functional units limit the graph size [@lee2022graphwave], and CoGraf reports gains for the region of interest (ROI) rather than end to end [@yenimol2026cograf]. On the DSA side, only ReGraph runs on real hardware in our corpus [@chen2022regraph]. We searched more widely for real-hardware PIM than for real-hardware DSAs (Section [2](#sec:background){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:background"}). This imbalance may therefore partly come from our search, and it does not show that DSAs avoid silicon.

## Cost, deployability, and programmability

The camps also differ in what they need from the rest of the system. A DSA is a component on the processor side. It can be an ASIC, a unit attached to each core [@mukkara2018hats; @zhao2022tdgraph], or an FPGA on an HBM card [@chen2022regraph], and the memory does not need to change. PIM needs new memory parts: HMC logic layers [@ahn2015tesseract], logic in the DIMM buffer chip [@talati2022ndminer], ReRAM crossbars [@song2018graphr], or modified DRAM dies with a significant area cost [@shin2025fala]. Of the PIM substrates surveyed, only UPMEM has been evaluated as a commercially available product [@gomezluna2022prim]. The hybrids are designed to keep the changes in memory small [@shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala].

Programmability follows the same pattern. DSAs keep a single shared address space and a vertex-programming interface that existing frameworks already support [@ham2016graphicionado]. Programmable PnM cores (Tesseract, UPMEM) also accept general code, but they leave partitioning, data placement, and communication to the programmer or the runtime [@ahn2015tesseract; @cai2024pimpam; @barkhordar2025alphapim]. ALPHA-PIM shows that the best partitioning format depends on the input density and the dataset. This is a tuning effort that DSAs do not need [@barkhordar2025alphapim]. Analog crossbars accept only SpMV-expressible kernels [@song2018graphr], and fine-grained PIM adds new types of instructions that compilers must support [@yenimol2026cograf]. In short, the deeper the compute is placed, the more of the graph's irregularity software must handle.

## A view by workload class

Organizing the evidence by workload class, instead of by camp, gives a more useful answer to the research question. In *whole-graph iterative kernels* such as PageRank, every vertex is active, and the access pattern is regular at the level of an iteration. Analog ReRAM and 3D-stacked PIM report their largest gains for these kernels [@song2018graphr; @challapalle2020gaasx; @ahn2015tesseract], and hybrids benefit from tiling here [@shin2025piccolo]. In *frontier-driven traversals* such as BFS and SSSP, the active set is small and changes in every iteration. Here, the burst-waste problem is worst for DSAs [@shin2025piccolo], and the synchronization problem is worst for real PIM [@gomezluna2022prim; @barkhordar2025alphapim]. Fine-grained hybrids target exactly this case [@shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf]. In *graph mining*, set operations over neighbor lists dominate the work, and partitions communicate little once the data is distributed. PIM shows strong results here on both simulated and real hardware [@besta2021sisa; @talati2022ndminer; @cai2024pimpam]. For *streaming and evolving* workloads, only DSAs offer hardware support [@rahman2021jetstream; @zhao2022tdgraph; @gao2023mega]. In short, the PIM evidence is strongest for mining and whole-graph kernels, only DSAs support dynamic workloads, and traversal is the open case that the hybrids are designed for. These are statements about where each camp has evidence, not results of matched comparisons.

## Which assumptions decide?

Table [\[tab:axes\]](#tab:axes){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:axes"} maps the evidence onto six assumptions. They play different roles. On current hardware, one of them separates the camps, one affects both camps, and one marks a gap in what the literature covers.

*Communication path (separates the camps).* PIM wins when partitions rarely communicate. Examples are pattern matching after partitioning [@cai2024pimpam], set operations [@besta2021sisa], and benchmarks without heavy synchronization [@gomezluna2022prim]. Its advantage becomes smaller on frontier-driven traversals that exchange data in every iteration, unless the design provides an interconnect between units [@zhuo2019graphq; @barkhordar2025alphapim]. On UPMEM, the only commercial platform evaluated, this exchange must pass through the host. No DSA has this constraint: a DSA's traversal traffic stays on chip or goes to memory over the normal interface, where it meets the external bandwidth limit instead [@orenesvera2023dalorex]. This assumption therefore separates the camps, but it decides *which* bottleneck each camp hits, not which camp is faster. Graph size is not a separate axis. It is usually presented as an argument for PIM, because PIM bandwidth grows with capacity [@ahn2015tesseract; @zhuo2019graphq]. However, the real-hardware results show that size helps only if communication does not grow with it [@gomezluna2022prim]. Size and communication therefore act together, and we treat them as one condition.

*Access granularity (affects both camps).* When each access brings little useful data, both conventional DSAs and coarse-grained PIM waste bandwidth [@shin2025piccolo]. Fine-grained PIM mechanisms help both camps [@shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf]. This assumption does not separate PIM from DSAs. It separates fine-grained from coarse-grained designs in either camp, and it explains why the camps are converging.

*Mutability (a gap in coverage).* Every PIM and hybrid hardware design we examined assumes a static graph [@ahn2015tesseract; @zhang2018graphp; @zhuo2019graphq; @song2018graphr; @ahn2015pei; @nai2017graphpim; @shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf]. A 2020 position paper already noted that earlier graph PIM "operated only on static graphs" and described the design considerations for dynamic graphs [@kim2020dynamic]. In our search of ISCA, MICRO, HPCA, and ASPLOS for 2021--2026, we found no PIM hardware design for streaming or evolving graphs. Outside these venues, a 2026 journal paper proposes a mapping approach for dynamic graph computation in computing-in-memory systems [@ghasemi2026dynamic]. A preprint also presents a PIM-based graph database that supports graph updates through dynamic partitioning [@ma2024moctopus]. We did not assess them: the first is outside our venue frame and we could not read its full text, and the second is a preprint. Their existence shows that the axis records what the top-venue literature covers, not what PIM can do. Among the surveyed works, the only PIM result that involves updates is the UPMEM triangle-counting study. It is a software study on existing PIM hardware, not a hardware design for dynamic graphs. It appends batches of new edges in COO format and counts the triangles that each batch forms. Its advantage over the CPU comes from avoiding a full CSR conversion after every update [@asquini2025tc]. The DSA camp, in contrast, has three generations of designs [@rahman2021jetstream; @zhao2022tdgraph; @gao2023mega]. We see three possible explanations for the gap. The first is convenience: PIM simulators and partitioners are built around static data layouts. The second is physical, and it applies to PIM that partitions the graph across memory units, such as Tesseract and UPMEM. There, the benefit depends on placing each partition's data in the unit that computes on it, and updates disturb this placement. An insertion can overflow a 64 MB DPU bank or make a partition unbalanced [@cai2024pimpam]. Kim and Kim list repartitioning as a necessary change for dynamic graphs [@kim2020dynamic], but it moves data through the narrow path that PIM is meant to avoid [@gomezluna2022prim]. The third concerns instruction-level PIM, where the host keeps the data layout: GraphPIM finds that graph updates need operations too complex for the simple atomics in memory [@nai2017graphpim]. The current evidence cannot decide between these explanations, and more than one may hold.

The remaining axes matter less. *Precision and programmability* separate analog ReRAM, which is limited to SpMV, from all other designs [@song2018graphr]. *Concurrency*, meaning many queries over one graph, has been addressed in software [@yin2023glign] but not by any surveyed hardware design.

# Properties for Critical Systems {#sec:critical}

Graph analytics supports applications such as network-security analysis [@asquini2025tc]. A network graph changes all the time, and a detector must deliver correct results within a deadline. This is the streaming setting where, within our search frame, only DSAs offer hardware support (Section [6](#sec:analysis){reference-type="ref" reference="sec:analysis"}). We therefore assess how the architecture of each camp affects three properties beyond performance (Table [\[tab:critical\]](#tab:critical){reference-type="ref" reference="tab:critical"}). We use these working definitions. *Security* means the confidentiality and integrity of graph data and graph structure against a stated adversary. *Dependability* means correct results despite hardware faults; we keep imprecision that is designed in, as in analog computation, separate from faults. *Predictability* means that the worst-case execution time (WCET) or latency can be bounded and analysed, not only that the average timing varies little.

*Security.* In the surveyed literature, the security of the two camps has been studied separately and never compared, and the two studies assume different adversaries. For DSAs, MGX protects against an attacker who can read or change off-chip memory. It shows that memory encryption and integrity protection can be adapted to the predictable data movement of an accelerator. Version numbers are generated from on-chip state. According to the authors, this reduces the protection overhead for graph accelerators from 33% to 5% [@hua2022mgx]. For PIM, IMPACT considers software that runs on the same system as the victim. It shows that PIM operations create high-throughput timing channels through the shared DRAM row buffer. Its two covert channels reach 8.2 and 14.8 Mb/s, which is $3.6\times$ and $6.5\times$ higher than earlier memory-based channels. Its side channel leaks information from victims that run at the same time. The authors evaluate the performance cost of their defenses on GraphBIG workloads, including BFS, connected components, and triangle counting [@bostanci2025impact]. A related analysis adds that processing-in-DRAM makes read disturbance worse: it reduces the number of accesses needed to cause the first bitflip by $158\times$ [@yuksel2026pid]. Because the adversaries differ, these results cannot be weighed against each other directly.

Two architectural points still hold. First, a DSA keeps the trust boundary at the memory controller, where conventional encryption can be used. PIM moves computation inside this boundary, where data must be processed as plaintext. Second, graph workloads make access patterns sensitive, because the access pattern itself encodes the graph. The sequence of rows or addresses that a traversal touches reveals adjacency and degree. This affects both camps. Encryption at the memory controller, as in MGX, protects data values but does not hide the address trace that an attacker on the memory bus can observe. In PIM, row-buffer timing channels like the ones IMPACT demonstrates could leak the same structure to co-located software. This is our own inference. IMPACT's side channel targets other applications, and no surveyed paper has evaluated graph-structure leakage, or a defense that hides access patterns, in either camp.

*Dependability.* GraphR justifies analog computation by arguing that iterative algorithms, and integer algorithms such as BFS and SSSP, tolerate imprecision [@song2018graphr]. However, none of the three ReRAM graph accelerators we surveyed evaluates device variation, stuck-at faults, or endurance. A full-text search of GraphR, Spara, and GaaS-X finds no such evaluation [@song2018graphr; @zheng2020spara; @challapalle2020gaasx]. This matters more for traversal than for PageRank. If a crossbar cell that stores the adjacency matrix is faulty, the topology of the graph itself changes. A single wrong edge can change the reachability results of BFS or SSSP, instead of only slightly changing a value that is converging. The read-disturbance problem mentioned above is also a reliability concern for digital PIM [@yuksel2026pid]. DSAs read and write conventional DRAM and on-chip SRAM, so standard error-correcting codes (ECC) protect data stored in DRAM. ECC does not cover the datapath or control logic, however, and on-chip scratchpads need their own protection. For PIM that computes inside DRAM, the computation happens below the level where standard ECC checks data, so ECC may not cover it. None of the surveyed DSA papers evaluates dependability either.

*Predictability.* No surveyed design in either camp analyses WCET or gives a latency bound. Graph traversal depends on the data by nature, but the architectures add their own variability. On UPMEM, every BFS iteration includes a frontier merge that runs serially on the host, and its cost grows as DPUs are added [@gomezluna2022prim]. Interference between concurrent queries from several tenants has only been studied as a throughput problem, in software [@yin2023glign]. Some DSA structures are more predictable by design. A scratchpad, such as the one in Graphicionado, has a fixed access latency, unlike a cache whose hit rate depends on the graph [@ham2016graphicionado]. On the other hand, asynchronous and event-driven execution makes the order of updates, and therefore the number of iterations, depend on timing [@rahman2021jetstream; @zhuo2019graphq]. For incremental DSAs, latency follows the size of the affected region, not the size of the graph [@rahman2021jetstream; @zhao2022tdgraph]. This is good on average. In the worst case, one update can affect the whole graph. The only obvious bound is then the cost of a full recomputation, which is finite but loose, and no surveyed paper analyses it. A design with bounded latency would need a bound on the work per update. It would also need bounded timing for every shared resource, such as the host merge on UPMEM or the memory controller. PIM adds a timing component that depends on the host and on the system scale.

# Open Challenges and Future Directions {#sec:open}

**1) PIM for dynamic graphs.** In the surveyed venues, the clearest gap is a PIM design that supports incremental updates without repeated repartitioning. There are several promising directions. Kim and Kim suggest keeping a log of edge and vertex changes in memory and merging it by streaming or in batches with explicit repartitioning [@kim2020dynamic]. Other options are kernels that work directly on COO data, as the UPMEM triangle-counting result suggests [@asquini2025tc], and hybrids that keep changing data on the host and stable partitions in PIM [@shin2025fala]. Open questions include the following. How large can an update batch be before repartitioning costs more than the bandwidth benefit of PIM? Can online locality scheduling, as in HATS [@mukkara2018hats], replace offline partitioning? Can multi-snapshot sharing, as in MEGA [@gao2023mega], work with data that is local to a bank?

**2) An interconnect between PIM units that does not go through the host.** Communication through the host is the main limit for traversal on real hardware [@gomezluna2022prim; @barkhordar2025alphapim], while the simulated designs that performed well assumed a direct interconnect [@ahn2015tesseract; @zhuo2019graphq]. Commercial PIM needs one before PIM traversal can match its simulated results. The key question for graphs is how much interconnect bandwidth frontier-heavy workloads need for each unit of internal bandwidth.

**3) Evaluation methodology.** One surveyed work could not reproduce another's simulated results [@orenesvera2023dalorex], so released simulators and configurations matter. The field also needs common CPU, GPU, and DSA baselines, end-to-end reporting instead of region-of-interest reporting [@yenimol2026cograf], and validation on real hardware or FPGA where possible [@shin2025piccolo; @chen2022regraph]. Energy should be reported at a common technology node and should include DRAM. PIM and DSA designs move energy between the memory and the processor, so each can look better when only part of the system is counted [@yenimol2026cograf; @ham2016graphicionado]. A shared evaluation package would allow the first direct comparison of PIM, DSA, and hybrid designs. It should include one graph suite, one set of algorithms, and both kernel and end-to-end times.

**4) Threat models that cover both camps.** Security has been studied separately for DSA memory protection [@hua2022mgx] and for PIM timing channels [@bostanci2025impact]. There is no comparative threat model for graph workloads, where the access patterns themselves reveal the graph structure. Such a model should cover both confidentiality (leakage of structure through timing) and integrity (bitflips caused by read disturbance in the stored adjacency data [@yuksel2026pid]). It should also measure the cost of defenses on graph kernels, as IMPACT started to do [@bostanci2025impact].

**5) Fault tolerance for analog graph PIM.** ReRAM graph accelerators need fault-injection studies before they can be used in critical systems [@song2018graphr; @zheng2020spara]. These studies should use metrics that fit traversal correctness, such as reachability errors, and not only numerical drift. Schemes that use the graph structure could detect faults at a lower cost than general-purpose ECC on analog arrays. Examples are replicating high-degree vertices, whose errors spread the furthest, and checking that SSSP distance labels only decrease.

**6) Programmability across hybrid designs.** Each hybrid introduces its own instructions or access modes [@shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf]. A shared abstraction that lets one graph program run on DSA pipelines, fine-grained PIM, and host caches would make each of these designs easier to adopt. The vertex-programming model already serves as a common front end for DSAs [@ham2016graphicionado], and linear-algebra formulations play the same role for PIM [@barkhordar2025alphapim]. A compiler could translate one of these models into code for each placement and decide where each phase should run. This would make hybrid designs usable for programmers.

# Conclusion

PIM and DSAs approach the memory wall of irregular graph workloads from opposite directions. We surveyed 34 works and compared them using an explicit rule for comparability. Because almost no study compares the two camps under matched conditions, we do not rank them. We report instead what each camp's evidence covers. The PIM evidence shows high internal bandwidth that grows with capacity, in simulation [@ahn2015tesseract; @zhuo2019graphq], and partly on silicon for workloads with little communication [@gomezluna2022prim; @cai2024pimpam]. On the one commercial platform, communication between units goes through the host, which limits traversal [@gomezluna2022prim; @barkhordar2025alphapim]. The simulated designs assumed a direct interconnect, so this difference reflects architecture as well as evaluation method. The DSA evidence shows flexibility and, in the venues we searched, the only hardware support for dynamic and evolving graphs [@rahman2021jetstream; @zhao2022tdgraph; @gao2023mega]. The limits of DSAs are external bandwidth and burst waste [@shin2025piccolo; @orenesvera2023dalorex], and they have almost no real-silicon validation. Both camps report large energy savings, but against different baselines. Their mechanisms differ, since DSAs remove general-purpose overheads and PIM removes external transfers, and the two mechanisms complement each other.

To answer the research question: graph size alone does not decide between the two approaches, because its benefit depends on communication. On current hardware, one assumption separates them: whether communication between units must pass through a host. Access granularity affects both camps and drives their convergence. Graph mutability marks where the evidence ends: in the venues we searched, only DSAs support changing graphs. The evidence therefore favors PIM for stable graphs with little traffic between partitions, and DSAs for evolving graphs, but these are statements about coverage, not results of matched comparisons. The hybrids of 2025--2026, which refine the host--memory cooperation of PEI and GraphPIM, suggest that the field is moving toward a combination of both [@ahn2015pei; @nai2017graphpim; @shin2025piccolo; @shin2025fala; @yenimol2026cograf]. The decisive study would be a hybrid that handles graph updates without repartitioning, evaluated end to end on real hardware against a DSA baseline. Such a study, with attention to security, dependability, and timing, would answer the question that this survey can only ask.
